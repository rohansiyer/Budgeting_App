Design system review · July 2026

Ducks in a Row, reviewed

Where the design system stands against Monarch, YNAB and Copilot, and what to change to win its actual audience: someone taking their first steps into financial independence. Pixel identity kept intact throughout; nothing here asks for a rounded corner.

01 · Verdict

The visual system is coherent, disciplined, and genuinely differentiated, a rarity. Zero radii, block meters, one accent, one shadow idiom, real copy rules: that's a stronger brand foundation than most funded competitors have. The problem is not the aesthetic. The problem is coverage: the system defines how a finished screen looks, but says nothing about the moments that decide whether a first-time budgeter survives week one, setup, empty states, data entry, and feeling progress.

Don't chase Monarch's breadth (sync, investments, net worth, couples). For your audience, that breadth is the weakness, feature-heavy dashboards overwhelm beginners and cost $100/yr. Compete on the three things Monarch structurally can't do: free + 100% private, one answer per day, and a game layer that makes consistency feel like something.

03 · Onboarding & first run
FIX NOW F1

The kit has no form layer at all

The setup wizard is a Play Store blocker in your own spec, yet the design system contains no text input, no numeric/amount field, no segmented choice, no toggle, no stepper, and no progress indicator. Thirteen components, zero of them accept typing. Every wizard screen will currently be improvised outside the system, which is exactly where the brand will erode first. Monarch's weakest moment is its 30–60 minute setup; a 4-step, 3-minute wizard is your single clearest chance to beat it, and it's undesigned.

Recommend: add a Field (48px tap height, surface-deep well, 1px border, mint border on focus, no glow), a ChoiceRow (segmented options built from ghost HardButtons), and a StepTrack progress element, blocks, not a bar, with the duck standing on the last filled block. Setup should feel like the game starting, not paperwork.

FIX NOW F2

Empty states don't exist, and you own the best empty-state asset in the category

Day one, the Home screen has no spend bars and no envelope history, and the pond holds exactly one duck: the starter. Every player begins with a flock of one, so the first screen is an introduction, not an apology. No pattern covers this yet. For a first-time budgeter, an empty dashboard reads as “I'm doing it wrong.” Every screen needs a defined zero state: what shows, what the duck does, and the one action to take next. The duck sprite makes these moments charming for free, competitors pay illustrators for what you already have.

04 · The daily loop
FIX NOW F3

Manual entry is your bank sync, design it like one

Staying offline is the right call, it's the trust story and the price story. But it means the add-expense flow carries the entire retention load that Plaid carries for Monarch. It deserves a dedicated, specified pattern: amount-first (a big mono keypad, not a form), recent categories as one-tap squares, live envelope feedback (“Food: $22.60 left after this”) before you confirm, and done in under five seconds. YNAB proved manual entry builds awareness; your job is making it cost nothing. This is also where the borrow prompt lives, the flow's one moment of drama, and today no component in the kit can render it. One rule change: borrowing is cadence-aware and open to every envelope. Each envelope lends from its own next cycle, weekly or monthly, whichever it runs; skip hard caps and let the honest math be the guardrail.

SOON F4

Home answers “can I buy this?” but not “am I okay?”

The hero number is the right bet, PocketGuard built a whole product on it. Two things are missing around it. First, pacing: $128.40 means nothing to a beginner without “4 days to payday · about $32 a day keeps you green.” Second, the month's stakes: the three duck goals are invisible until you visit the Pond tab. The emotional engine, the one thing no competitor has, is hidden on tab three and pays out once a month. Put a quiet goal strip on Home so every day is a move in the game, and warnings arrive while the month's duck is still earnable.

05 · Insights: match them, minus the friction
FIX NOW F5

Match their insight, free, and delivered instead of fetched

Decision: Ducks offers the insight the $100/yr apps sell, where the money went, how this month compares, what's drifting, what's recurring, for free, computed entirely on-device. Nothing about good insight requires a server: trends, pacing, month-over-month deltas, and recurring-spend detection are arithmetic on numbers the user already typed in. “The paid apps' reports, free and private” is the sharpest single answer to why not just pay for Monarch?

The differentiator isn't the math, it's the friction. Monarch's insight lives behind a reports tab, filters, and a scheduled money date; YNAB's behind a methodology course. The rule for Ducks: insights are delivered, never fetched. One rotating sentence on Home computed at open, live envelope math inside add-expense (“$22.60 left after this”), and a recap that builds itself at the close of every pay period (month-end adds the duck verdict), the user never runs a report; the report visits them. And cap it hard, one insight per surface per day, so it stays one answer a day instead of becoming the dashboard sprawl you're escaping. The cap applies to what's pushed, not what's reachable, see F10 below. Two patterns carry it:

InsightRow, a category chip + one plain-language sentence with the exact dollar figure, in the existing voice. SparkBlocks, a six-month trend as columns of discrete blocks (money stays countable; never a smooth line chart). Both slot into the Results screen, turning “Time to count ducks!” into a real recap, the habit Monarch users call the money date, in your language, and the natural screen to share.

On writing the sentences: this is not prose generation. Each InsightRow is a deterministic rule, a trigger condition plus a template with slots ({category} came in {amount} under budget, your best month since {month}). Write ~20 rules once, rank them, and each month the top three that fired get rendered. If only one fired, show one, silence beats filler, and an insight that isn't true for this user never appears. No AI, no server, an afternoon of copywriting rather than an engine.

SOON F10

Every number is a door, the savvy user sees everything

Some users are genuinely savvy, they just also want ducks. Serve them with progressive disclosure, not a mode: the surface stays one-answer-a-day, but every number on it opens to its own math. Tap safe-to-spend → the exact ledger that produced it (income in, bills reserved, envelopes funded, savings set aside). Tap an envelope → every transaction, carryover history, borrow record. Tap an insight sentence → the numbers behind it. One tap down from anything, nothing hidden, nothing simplified away.

This costs almost nothing to design: it's all RuledList, full-bleed hairline rows, tabular numerals, exact dollars, the treatment the system already reserves for anything read as a list. Add a plain CSV export in Settings (their data, on their device, theirs to take) and the honesty principle the copy already promises, “every dollar is accounted for; nothing is ever invented or lost”, becomes something the user can verify, not just read. That's a claim none of the three subscription apps can make as cleanly, and it never bothers the user who only ever wants the duck.

06 · Type, hierarchy & accessibility
SOON F6

The scale jumps 56 → 24 → 14 · the middle is missing

There is no size between a screen title and body copy, so every mid-importance number, envelope remainders, day KPIs, sheet titles, goal amounts, renders at 14px and screens read flatter than they are. Copilot's perceived polish is mostly a well-tuned mid-scale. Add one token: --text-kpi: 20px / 700. It's the cheapest polish win in this document.

SOON F7

Small-text floor and focus states

11.5px captions and 11px labels are at the floor of comfortable mobile legibility, fine as labels, risky wherever they carry real content (the pacing line, insight sentences, warnings should sit at 13–14px). Raise the caption token to 12px and reserve 11px for tracked uppercase labels only. Separately, the system defines press states but no focus state, the moment a web or desktop surface exists (and this design system itself is one), keyboard focus needs a spec. A 1px mint outline offset by 2px is enough and stays in-brand.

Worth saying plainly: the accessibility fundamentals here beat the competitors', 48px minimum targets, color-never-alone with mandatory text fallbacks, CVD-validated category hues, and reduced-motion enforced in source rather than promised. Guard these; they're marketing claims as much as compliance.

08 · Copy & tone
GUARD F8

The voice is a genuine competitive asset, extend it to the hard moments

“A tough month. One duck waddles off, but the pond is still here for you” is better writing than anything in Monarch, which speaks fluent dashboard. But the copy system only covers the happy paths. Missing: error copy (bad import, failed backup, invalid amount), notification copy (a bill reminder in this voice is an asset; a generic one breaks the spell), and a celebration library (the good-month strings will repeat within three months, write 6–8 variants per event now). One rule worth codifying while extending: warnings name the duck at stake; errors never do, the game never blames you for a technical failure.

09 · Product-level gaps vs the field
SOON F9

Pick the fights that fit the audience

Named goals. Monarch's most-loved feature after reports, and the most natural extension of your envelope model. “First month's rent for my own place: $340 of $1,200” as a PixelBox with a BlockMeter is more motivating for this audience than any net-worth chart, and it's nearly free to build. This is the one Monarch feature worth taking.

A home-screen widget. Deferred in the spec, but for a manual-entry app the widget is the retention mechanism: safe-to-spend + duck status on the lock screen keeps the daily question answered without opening the app. Reprioritize above notifications polish.

Privacy as the headline, backup as the safety net. “No accounts, no tracking, nothing leaves your phone” is the loudest differentiator against three Plaid-dependent subscriptions, lead the store listing with it. Its cost is that losing a phone means losing the flock, so backup nudges (quarterly, gentle, in-voice) belong in the design system as a pattern, not just a settings row.

SOON F11

The data layer: import, forecast, detect, search

CSV import with semantic category matching. The one thing sync buys competitors is history, so let users bring their own. Matching stays on-device: merchant strings tokenized and matched against category keyword lists, plus everything learned from the user's own corrections (assign “TRADER JOE'S” to Food once, it's Food forever). Imports always land in a review screen, “We sorted 142 of 151. Check these 9.”, never silently, per the honesty principle. Payoff: a year of history on day one means the insight layer works in week one, not month three.

Bills forecast. “What's due before payday” as a next-14-days RuledList, feeding the safe-to-spend math it already implies. Subscription detection is the same trigger machinery as the insight rules, a recurring merchant at a steady amount fires a sentence (“Spotify hit for the 3rd month, $11.99. Still using it?”) and offers to mark it as a bill. Search & filter ships with the F10 ledger: a Field on top of a RuledList, filter by category chip, mono numerals. All four are one design language, no new visual ideas required.

SPICY F12

Long-term trends and a predicted balance, yes, with two house rules

Rule one: stepped, never smooth. A smooth line chart breaks the “money stays countable” principle, but a step line (one step per week, hard right angles, pixel-true) is native to this brand, the chart equivalent of steps() motion. Rule two: projections borrow the pond's transparency device. Actual balance draws solid; the predicted continuation draws at 45% opacity, labeled “if your last 3 months hold”, so an invented number is never rendered as a fact. The prediction itself is honest arithmetic, not ML: median trailing spend per envelope plus scheduled bills, projected forward. That's enough to answer the questions this audience actually has, when do I hit $1,000? Can I afford September?

Don't build: bank sync, investment tracking, net worth, couples/household, AI chat. Each one drags the product toward the incumbents' home turf, adds accounts/servers that break the privacy story, and serves a user Ducks isn't for. The moment your user has a brokerage account, let them graduate to Monarch, on good terms.

10 · What not to change

Everything in this review builds on these; competitive pressure is not a reason to soften any of them:

Zero corner radius BlockMeter over progress bars One accent, semantic amber/coral No emoji, no icon library Stepped motion only Undo, never confirm dialogs Exact dollars, warm feelings 100% offline & free

Three flags to carry forward: no logo/app icon exists (blocking store assets, the duck sprite is the obvious starting point); Space Grotesk/Mono is a substitution the team should ratify or replace; and the duck is now a mallard, not a rubber duck, same 14×12 pixel grid and animation frames, new palette (green head, brown breast, white collar), a sprite-data recolor rather than a new asset, which also hands yellow back to the semantic palette.
